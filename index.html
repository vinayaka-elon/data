Business Data Finder

Public datasets for the MGT 3100 final project, across finance, retail, marketing, housing, transportation, energy, government, sports and more: what is in each one, what one row is, what it takes to get access, the trap to watch for, and the code to load it. Pick a topic, then narrow by the models the data supports, by level, by source or by access.

How to use this page, read this before you pick a project

1. Write the question first. One sentence, naming what you are predicting and from what. "Do neighborhoods with more Airbnb listings have faster rent growth?" Write the question before browsing, so the data serves the question.

2. Pick one topic. One chip only. The list collapses to the sources that could answer a question in that area.

3. Filter Access to Open first. Open needs no account and no waiting. Registration means a free account or a free API key you can get today. Application and Purchase take weeks or cost money.

4. Filter Models and Level. Regression needs a numeric outcome, Classification a yes/no or category, Time series a value tracked over time, Text a column of words. Beginner entries are one clean file; Advanced entries need an API, joins or heavy cleaning.

5. Open the entry and read in this order: the gold warning first, then Unit, which tells you what one row is, then Variables to confirm your outcome exists, then How to get it and the copy-paste starter. Star the entries worth keeping and use Shortlist to compare them.

6. Save the file you analyze. Several sources update continuously, so a live URL will not match what you wrote earlier. Save the file, record the date, and cite your saved copy.

7. Two sources at most. If you join, the entry names the key, usually a county FIPS code, a state code, a ticker, a ZIP code or a date. Read ID columns as str, or a leading zero disappears and the join silently returns nothing. Always print the row count before and after:

df = a.merge(b, on="fips", how="inner")
print(len(a), len(b), "->", len(df))   # if this is 0, check your dtypes

Before you commit to a source, confirm three things: you can download it today without asking anyone; one row is the unit you actually want to model; and the outcome you plan to predict is a real column. If any of the three is shaky, pick again now.

Seven things to know before picking one

  1. Access tier is the schedule risk. Open data loads today. A free API key usually arrives within minutes, sometimes a day. Application and Purchase tiers can take weeks, so check the badge before you fall for a dataset.
  2. Aggregates hide people. County, state and monthly tables describe places and periods. A pattern across counties says nothing certain about the individuals inside them, so match your conclusions to the unit of the row.
  3. Time series break the usual split. For prices, sales or traffic over time, train on the earlier periods and test on the later ones. A random split lets the model peek at the future.
  4. Nominal dollars mislead. Prices, wages and revenue across years need adjusting for inflation (CPI from FRED or BLS) before you compare them.
  5. Survivors only. Stock lists, active listings and current employees leave out the firms that failed, the homes that sold and the people who quit. Ask who is missing from the rows.
  6. Live sources change. APIs and portals revise past values and add new ones. Save the extract you analyze and record the date.
  7. Teaching datasets teach the method. Classic files such as tips or penguins are excellent for learning a pipeline and too small or too clean for a business claim. Use them to practice, then move to real data.

Final project

Your group answers one business question with public data, end to end: define the problem, find the data, clean it, describe it, test it, model it, and then hand the same analysis to an AI assistant and judge how well it did. Groups of three or four. The question is yours.

The one-sentence test. Write your project as a single sentence naming what you are predicting and what you are predicting it from. "Do neighborhoods with more Airbnb listings have faster rent growth?" "Which bank customers subscribe to a term deposit after a marketing call?" If you cannot write that sentence yet, you have a topic and still need a question.

1Write the sentence. What you predict, from what. Write it before opening the catalog so the question drives the data choice.
2Go to Find data and pick one topic. The list collapses to the sources that could answer a question in that area.
3Filter Access to Open, and Level to Beginner or Intermediate. Then filter Models: Regression if your outcome is a number, Classification if it is yes/no.

What you hand in

  • A notebook that runs. Restart the kernel, run all, no errors, top to bottom.
  • Your saved data file with the date you downloaded it.
  • A short write-up: question, data and cleaning, methods, results, AI evaluation, limitations. Every number in it appears in a notebook output.
  • A presentation: one question, one chart that carries the finding, one honest limitation, and one lesson from the AI evaluation.

How projects fail

  • No outcome variable. "We'll explore trends in X" produces charts and no project. Name the column you are predicting first.
  • Running dropna() without looking. The rows that go missing are often the ones that matter most. Compare dropped rows against kept rows before deleting anything.
  • Reporting accuracy on an imbalanced outcome. If 90% of rows are "No", a model that always says "No" scores 90%. Report the baseline next to your accuracy.
  • Leaking the answer. If a predictor is computed from the outcome, or recorded after it, the model is reading the answer key. Drop it and say why.
  • Claiming causation. Public data is observational. "Associated with" is the strongest phrase available.
  • Analyzing a live URL. The numbers change under you and the write-up stops matching the notebook.
  • Trusting the AI's numbers. An assistant can report a coefficient or a p-value it never computed. Every AI claim gets checked against your notebook.

Worth more than a clean result

A limitation you found yourself, specific to your data: two sources that define the same variable differently, a sample that excludes the customers who left, a column that changed meaning halfway through the years. A negative result, reported straight: if your predictor does nothing, say so and say what you would need to detect an effect. A well-executed null finding earns full credit.

Project launch workshop

Sixty minutes. Every group leaves with three things: a one-sentence question, a dataset that loaded on their own laptop, and a named outcome column. Nothing else is the goal, and nothing less is acceptable.

Built for 10 groups of three. Laptops required. Start the clock when you start talking.

0:00 – 0:051. Frame the gate

Open the Project guide tab and read the one-sentence test aloud. Put one good sentence and one bad one on screen side by side.

"Do neighborhoods with more Airbnb listings have faster rent growth?", that is a project. "We want to look at Airbnb", that is a topic. By the end of this hour every group here has the first kind, a dataset open on a laptop, and the name of the column you are predicting. If you leave with anything less, you will be behind for the rest of the term.

State the two hard rules now so nobody argues later: one or two sources, and Open or Registration access only.

0:05 – 0:122. Silent three

No talking. Every student, individually, writes three candidate questions on paper. Each one must name a thing being predicted and a thing predicting it.

Why silent. In a group of three, whoever speaks first sets the agenda and the other two start editing. Seven minutes of silence puts nine ideas on the table. Enforce it, the room will try to start talking at minute two.

Circulate but do not answer questions yet. If someone is stuck, point at one industry, one city, or one kind of customer and move on.

0:12 – 0:223. Converge to one

Groups read all nine aloud, then cut on two criteria only:

  1. Can you name the outcome column, or at least say what kind of number it is?
  2. Does data for this plausibly exist and is anyone allowed to have it?

Pick one. Write the sentence on the card. Write the runner-up down too, they will need it in phase 4 more often than they expect.

Watch for: groups picking the most interesting question over the most answerable one. Interesting is cheap. Ask them which one they could still be working on at the end of the term.
0:22 – 0:374. Data hunt, laptops open

Send them to the Find data tab. One topic chip, Access set to Open, Models set to whatever they intend to fit, Level at Beginner or Intermediate. Open two or three entries, read the gold warning first, then copy the starter code and run it.

The gate for this phase is a printed output. Not a bookmark, not a plan to download later. They must see df.shape and their outcome column in a cell output before the clock runs out.

Fifteen minutes. If the code has not run by the end of it, you switch to your backup question. That is not failure, that is the hour doing its job. Finding out now costs you fifteen minutes. Finding out late in the term costs you the project.
Watch for: a group three levels deep in an Application or Purchase dataset. Check the access badge over their shoulder and redirect immediately.
0:37 – 0:475. Red team, both directions

Pair the groups, five pairs. Five minutes each direction, and the clock splits it for you.

The reviewing group works down the checklist out loud. The presenting group may not defend itself, they listen and write. That rule is the whole exercise; say it twice.

Red-team checklist, read each one aloud

  1. Say their sentence back to them. If you cannot, they do not have one.
  2. Name their outcome column. Point at it in their output. If it is not on screen, it does not exist.
  3. What is one row? A customer, a store, a county, a day? If the group hesitates, flag it.
  4. Check the access badge. Open or Registration? If it is Application or Purchase, stop and say so now.
  5. How many sources? More than two, or no join key named for the second, flag it.
  6. Does any predictor give away the answer? Is one column the thing the outcome was computed from?
  7. Read their gold warning out loud. Ask what they are going to do about it. "Nothing" is a real answer only if they can say why.
  8. What would a boring result look like? If they cannot describe one, they are planning to find what they already believe.
0:47 – 0:556. Revise while I circulate

Groups fix what the red team found. You go table to table, 45 seconds each, which is exactly enough for ten groups with slack.

Ask one question at every table and then move:

Show me the output where your outcome column appears.

Everything else can wait for office hours. If the answer is "we are about to", the group is behind and you should say so before you walk away.

0:55 – 1:007. Commit out loud

Each group stands and gives twenty seconds: the sentence, the dataset name, and the number of rows. No slides, no preamble, no apologising. Ten groups fits in four minutes if you hold the line.

Collect the cards on the way out.

Why out loud. A sentence said in front of nine other groups is harder to quietly abandon next week than one written on a form. This is the point of the last five minutes, do not let it get cut when the hour runs long.

Before the room walks in

  • Send this page the night before, pointed at the Project guide tab. Ask them to arrive with one question already in mind, the silent phase goes better when it is not cold.
  • Check that pandas loads on the room's wifi. Half of phase 4 dies on a proxy, not on the data.
  • Print the project card. Paper is faster than a form and you can read ten of them in the lift.
  • Have three fallback datasets ready for groups who stall: UCI Bank Marketing, Ames Housing, Hotel booking demand. All three are one file, open, and load in a single line.

What success looks like at 1:00

Ten cards in your hand, each with a sentence you could repeat back, a dataset name, a row count and a column name. Perhaps two groups will have switched to their backup question, and that is the hour working as intended. What you should not have is a group still deciding between three topics, if you see that at 0:47, give them your pick and move on. They can change it later; they cannot get the week back.
